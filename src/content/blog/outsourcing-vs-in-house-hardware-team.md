Every hardware company eventually asks: should we build our own electronics team, or work with an external development partner? The answer is rarely all-or-nothing, and the right choice depends on more than hourly rates.

As an external team ourselves, we have an obvious perspective. So instead of a sales pitch, here is the comparison we would want to read if we were on the other side of the table.

## What a complete product team actually needs

A connected electronics product, taken from concept to mass production, typically requires:

- Electronics and PCB design (including power, analog, RF where relevant)
- Embedded firmware (drivers, RTOS, connectivity, bootloader/OTA)
- Application and cloud software, or at least integration with it
- Mechanical design and enclosure tooling
- Test engineering and production support
- Compliance (EMC, safety, radio) and documentation
- Supply chain and contract manufacturer management

Few early-stage companies need all of these full-time, but almost every product needs all of them at some point.

## The real cost of an in-house team

Salary is the visible part. The full cost includes:

- **Recruiting time:** experienced hardware and firmware engineers can take months to hire, and each month is a month of delayed development
- **Fully loaded cost:** benefits, taxes, equipment, office space and management overhead typically add a significant percentage on top of base salary
- **Tools:** professional ECAD and MCAD licenses, debuggers, oscilloscopes, spectrum analyzers, power analyzers and lab equipment
- **Coverage gaps:** a two-person team rarely covers RF, power electronics, EMC, mechanics and production test with equal depth
- **Utilization:** hardware work is lumpy; between design phases, specialist capacity can sit idle or be assigned to work outside their strength

## The real cost of outsourcing

External development also has costs beyond the invoice:

- **Knowledge transfer:** if the partner does not document well, knowledge leaves with them
- **Communication overhead:** time zones, requirements writing, and review cycles
- **Alignment risk:** a partner optimizing for billable hours instead of your outcome
- **IP and confidentiality:** handled with clear contracts and NDAs, but it needs attention
- **Less day-to-day control:** you see progress at review points rather than at the next desk

## When does an in-house hardware team make sense?

- Hardware is your **core differentiator** and you will iterate on it continuously for years
- You have **steady, predictable workload** across multiple product lines
- You can **attract and retain** senior talent in the disciplines you need
- You have time to build the team before the critical development phase

## When does outsourcing make sense?

- You need to **reach market quickly** and cannot wait months to hire
- The workload is **project-shaped**: intense development followed by sustaining engineering
- You need **breadth of experience**, such as having seen many products go through DFM, EMC and certification
- You want to **validate the product** before committing to a permanent team

## The hybrid model we see working best

In practice, many successful hardware companies combine both:

1. **A small in-house core** that owns product requirements, architecture decisions, and key IP
2. **An external partner** that brings specialist depth and capacity for development peaks, from design through production ramp
3. **Structured knowledge transfer**, with documented designs, version-controlled sources, and review sessions, so the in-house team can sustain and evolve the product

This gives you control over what matters most, access to experience you could not hire quickly, and costs that follow the actual workload.

## Questions to ask any development partner

- Have you taken products **all the way to mass production**, not just prototypes?
- Who will actually work on our project, and will that stay consistent?
- How do you **document and hand over** designs, sources and manufacturing files?
- Who owns the IP, and how is confidentiality handled from the first conversation?
- How do you handle scope changes and estimate uncertainty?
- Can you cover hardware, firmware, software and mechanical design, or will we be coordinating several vendors?

A good partner will answer these clearly and expect you to ask them.

## A simple way to compare

Build a 12–24 month model of both options with:

- Time to first prototype and to production for each option
- Fully loaded cost per month, including tools and management time
- The cost of delay: revenue or funding milestones pushed out by slower hiring
- Risk items: missing expertise, key-person dependency, respins

For many companies, the cost of delay dominates every other line.
